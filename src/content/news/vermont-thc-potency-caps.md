Vermont is one of the few adult-use states that writes a hard ceiling on THC into statute. Flower cannot exceed 30% THC. Solid concentrates cannot exceed 60%. The rule arrived with Act 164, the 2020 law that created the regulated retail market.

## What this changes in practice

Walk into a dispensary in a state without caps and you will find concentrates in the 80-90% range. You will not find them in Vermont. The concentrate category here is compressed into a narrower band, and product categories that depend on very high potency are simply absent from menus.

For flower, the cap bites less often than people assume. Most flower on the market tests below 30% anyway. Where it matters is at the top end, with the modern high-testing cultivars that occasionally push past the line.

## The argument for it

The reasoning behind the cap was harm reduction, particularly for inexperienced users and for younger adults. Vermont's legislature took the position that a legal market does not have to be an unlimited one.

## The argument against it

Critics make two points worth taking seriously. The first is that caps push high-potency demand to the unregulated market, where there is no testing at all. The second is that potency is a poor proxy for effect. A 25% flower with a heavy myrcene profile can feel stronger than a 30% flower without it, and the cap does not capture that.

## What to do with this as a buyer

Read the certificate of analysis rather than the headline THC number. It lists cannabinoid content and, on most Vermont products, a terpene breakdown. The terpene profile does more to predict how something will feel than the THC percentage does.

If a budtender steers you toward a lower-THC product with a specific terpene profile, that is usually good advice rather than an upsell.
